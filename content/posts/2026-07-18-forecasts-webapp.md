A new MOU with the teachers' union, a reduction in force, a school consolidation: every one of these proposals ends up as a spreadsheet with a number at the bottom, and the conversation about it almost always starts and ends with that number. What rarely gets written down is what the proposal actually changes for students and staff: how many sections get combined, which positions or services are affected, what a step-and-column schedule means for a specific group of employees. That description usually lives in someone's head or in an unlabeled spreadsheet cell, if it exists anywhere at all. The dollar figure stands in for the whole analysis instead of being what it actually is: one consequence of programmatic changes nobody wrote down. When a board member asks what happens if enrollment comes in lower, or a union negotiator asks where a figure came from, the honest answer is usually a new spreadsheet, not a description of what was assumed.

This isn't a new observation.[^bias] There are real incentives to leave assumptions vague. A fuzzy forecast is harder to argue with than a precise, checkable one. Even without any intent to obscure anything, most budget spreadsheets simply weren't built to describe a proposal before they price it. So I built [a web application](https://forecasts.ebardelli.com) that inverts the usual order: instead of starting from a dollar figure and working backward, it makes you describe and quantify a proposal's impact first, as labeled assumptions, and only then generates the financial forecast from them automatically.

[^bias]: I touched on a version of this problem in an [earlier post on enrollment projections](/posts/enrollment-projections/#systemic-and-behavioral-biases): research has found that districts sometimes intentionally bias revenue and expenditure projections to build budget slack, and that this is easier to get away with when the assumptions behind a projection aren't stated explicitly enough for anyone outside the finance office to check.

## Fiscal Forecasting Shouldn't Be the Goal

The financial forecast that comes out of the tool is useful, but it's meant to be a *consequence* of a clearer exercise, not the goal of it. The goal is to estimate the educational impact of a specific proposal first, and then calculate the financial forecast after. The forecast is what you get once you have a good idea of what a project will actually change. If you only care about the bottom-line number, you can get that from a spreadsheet in five minutes. What a spreadsheet won't give you is a structure where someone else can open the analysis, see exactly which assumptions drove the number, disagree with one of them, and see how much that disagreement actually matters to the outcome. That last part, testing how much a single disagreement moves the bottom line, used to mean rebuilding the spreadsheet. Now it's a named scenario you can build in a couple minutes and compare directly against the baseline.

## How it works

The app is organized around three ideas, each with its own page in the sidebar: assumptions, projects, and settings. A Home page ties them together, opening on a getting-started guide and one-click sample projects for a new user, and on an overview of projects, outstanding disclosure to-dos, and backup status once there's data.

**Assumptions.** You define reusable *units*, small, named pieces of a fiscal claim, like "step increase cost per FTE" or "Health & Welfare benefit rate." Each unit is built from one-off values, cells pulled from an assumption table (typed in, pasted from Excel, or uploaded as a workbook), or other units, and each value can carry an uncertainty (a normal, bounded, triangular, lognormal, or other distribution) rather than pretending to be exact. Each unit can also be tagged with a California SACS expenditure object code, the same codes a district's budget already uses, from which the app derives its cost category (personnel, materials, facilities, and so on, following the Institute of Education Sciences' Ingredients Method) and, for compensation, its line on the AB 1200 disclosure. Because units can reference other units, a complicated claim like "total cost of the proposed raise" is composed out of smaller, individually labeled assumptions instead of being a single opaque number no one can unpack.

**Projects.** You create one or more *projects* (the MOU, the reduction proposal, the consolidation), each carrying a participant count, and attach units to it as line items with a quantity and a payer/perspective tag (district, state, grant, family, and so on) marking who actually bears that cost. A line item can also be a percentage of other line items, so statutory benefits can be entered as a rate on salaries and follow every change to them. Units are shared and defined once, so if a benefits load rate assumption changes, every project referencing it updates together, instead of drifting apart across a dozen one-off spreadsheets built for separate board items. A project's own line items are its business-as-usual (BAU) baseline, but a real budget conversation is never about just one version of the future. From there you can branch off named scenarios (say, "vendor price increase," "lower step movement," "enrollment comes in 5% low") that override just the pieces that scenario changes, a specific cost component, a single line item's quantity, price, or uncertainty, or add a brand-new line item BAU never had. A scenario can also build on another one, so Year 2 of an agreement starts from Year 1 rather than from scratch. Everything you don't override stays tied to the shared baseline. Running a scenario simulates possible outcomes based on the uncertainty values you select, returning useful distribution information (i.e., mean, median, P5/P10/P90/P95, a histogram, per project and overall) alongside how much it costs *above* BAU, all side by side with a synthetic Baseline scenario that carries no overrides at all. That's what makes it a sensitivity analysis and not just another forecast: you can see exactly how much a specific disagreement (e.g., one assumption, one price, one uncertainty band) moves the outcome.

Each project opens on a Summary page where you pick BAU or a scenario for each of the next three years and see the window's expected cost, its range, and where the money goes by category, payer, and line item. A "Download scenario report" button exports every saved scenario plus Baseline into one comparison workbook: a summary sheet ranking them side by side, a breakdown by payer and cost category, a cost-per-participant figure, and the full distribution behind each.

Projects can also turn on optional modules. **Personnel** costs a salary schedule directly from a headcount on each step and column, and lets scenarios change staffing and pay the way an agreement does: step advancement, attrition, raises, or a new schedule. **AB 1200** walks through California's public disclosure of a collective bargaining agreement, mapping each agreement year to a scenario and exporting the county's disclosure workbook with the figures filled in.

**Settings.** Backup, restore, and reset live here, out of the way of the day-to-day pages, along with the default uncertainty for each cost category and the toggle for the getting-started guide. Exporting a full backup downloads every unit, table, project, line item, and scenario (including all its overrides) as an Excel workbook; importing that file back in restores the whole database exactly, and a separate reset action wipes everything to start over. That matters for a fiscal analysis in particular. The assumptions behind a multi-year MOU or a facilities decision often need to survive staff turnover and get revisited next budget cycle, rather than get rebuilt from scratch. The backup workbook is distinct from a scenario report: the backup round-trips everything back into the app, while a scenario report is a read-only comparison meant for sharing with a board or a bargaining team.

All of this runs entirely client-side. Data lives in a real [DuckDB](https://duckdb.org) database via [`@duckdb/duckdb-wasm`](https://github.com/duckdb/duckdb-wasm), persisted to the browser's Origin Private File System, and nothing is sent to a server. For fiscal analysis tied to ongoing labor negotiations, that's not incidental. It's why people are willing to put sensitive numbers into the tool in the first place. It works best in a Chromium-based browser (Chrome, Edge); Firefox and Safari have partial support for that storage feature and fall back to an in-memory session that doesn't persist across reloads.

## App Updates

### October 2026

- **AB 1200 public disclosure** adds an optional per-project module that walks through the county's Government Code 3547.5 disclosure form for a bargaining agreement as a seven-step guided flow, from agreement details and narrative explanations to current-year fund budgets, the multiyear projection, and reserves. Each agreement year maps to a scenario, costs are split into the form's compensation lines (salary schedule, step and column, statutory benefits, health and welfare, and so on), and the export is a complete disclosure workbook with every derived cell written as a live formula. Each step lists what it still needs, so missing fields are easy to find before the form goes to the board.
- **SACS object codes** replace the separate cost-category and compensation-line tags: units and line items are now tagged with a California SACS expenditure object (1100 for certificated teachers' salaries, 3101 for STRS, 4300 for materials, and so on), and the Ingredients Method category and AB 1200 compensation line are derived from it. That also lets the disclosure fill in the current-year budget's settlement column by budget row, so salaries land on the certificated or classified row by their object code and non-compensation costs get rows too. Existing databases and older backups are translated automatically, with any ambiguous guesses flagged for review.
- **Personnel module** costs a pay scale directly: upload a salary schedule as an assumption table, enter a headcount for each step and column, and the whole group becomes one line item instead of a unit per cell. Scenarios can then change staffing and pay the way an agreement actually does, through step advancement with column movement, attrition with replacement, across-the-board, row, or column raises, a swapped schedule, or individual cell overrides. Classified and management scales that step across columns rather than down rows are supported too, and allocations can be downloaded as a workbook for HR to fill in and uploaded back with a preview before anything is saved.
- **Percentage line items** let a line item be a percentage of one or more others, or of everything tagged with a compensation line (statutory benefits as 23% of salaries, say), so rate-based costs follow every scenario change to what they're based on without being folded into the salary unit itself.
- **Scenarios that build on other scenarios** make multi-year agreements easier to model: Year 2 can start from Year 1 instead of from BAU, with edits to Year 1 flowing through automatically, raises compounding, and one-time payments dropping off. Scenarios can also be duplicated outright.
- **Project Summary page** lets you pick BAU or a scenario for each of three years and see the window's expected cost and P5–P95 range, its distribution, breakdowns by category, payer, and largest line items, and a one-year comparison of BAU against every scenario.
- **Home page and sample projects** replace the empty first screen with a getting-started guide and a one-click sample: a reading intervention program and a two-year teachers' agreement with its AB 1200 disclosure filled in. Once there's data, Home shows projects at a glance, outstanding disclosure to-dos, and backup status.
- **Assumption table workbooks** download every assumption table as an Excel workbook and upload an edited copy back, updating cells in place so units and personnel allocations keep pointing at the same rows.

### September 2026

- **Uncertainty overhaul** replaces the scattered statistical option fields on components, line items, and quantities with one shared `Uncertainty` editor: an "Add uncertainty" button opens a modal with a kind picker, that kind's parameters, and a live closed-form preview of the resulting distribution. The registry now spans seven functional forms, normal, bounded, delta (a guaranteed shift with no randomness), uniform, triangular, lognormal, and PERT, with the last two sampled through DuckDB's stochastic extension.
- **Category-default uncertainty and a sensitivity breakdown** let a default `Uncertainty` be configured per cost category in Settings, applied automatically to Baseline and to any scenario that doesn't override a given item, so Baseline is no longer artificially risk-free. A new tornado-style breakdown ranks a project's line items by their exact closed-form contribution to variance, without any extra simulation trials.
- **Desktop app** packages the same client-side tool as a native download for macOS, Linux, and Windows, alongside the browser version. It's not published anywhere yet; email hello@ebardelli.com if you'd like a copy.

### August 2026

- **Backup-reminder toast** surfaces the same overdue-backup check that previously lived only on the Settings tab as a dismissible toast visible from any tab, since app data lives only in browser-local storage with no server-side copy.

### July 2026

- **Ingredients Method alignment** brings the domain model in line with the Institute of Education Sciences' *Cost Analysis: A Starter Kit* (IES 2020-001). Units now carry a cost category (personnel, materials, facilities, etc.), line items carry a payer/perspective tag (district, state, grant, family, and so on), and projects carry a participant count. A scenario report now breaks its cost down by payer and by category, and adds a cost-per-participant figure, alongside the existing distribution stats.
- **Projects tab** merges what used to be separate Planning and Scenarios tabs into one project-centric view. A project's own line items are now explicitly its business-as-usual (BAU) baseline, and every scenario belongs to a single project instead of floating as a global, cross-project entity. Scenarios can also add brand-new line items BAU never had, not just override existing ones.
- **Incremental cost vs. BAU** is now reported for every scenario: its own distribution (expected value, mean/median, P5/P10/P90/P95, histogram) alongside how much it costs *above* the project's baseline, computed as an exact shift of the scenario's own simulation rather than a second Monte Carlo pass, since BAU carries no uncertainty of its own.
